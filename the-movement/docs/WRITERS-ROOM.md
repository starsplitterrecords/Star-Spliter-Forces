# THE MOVEMENT: The Writers' Room

This is the working document for the creative production of THE MOVEMENT. The writing bible (`WRITING-BIBLE.md`) says *how* to write a card. This document says *what* we are writing, *who* is funny and why, and what we have already tried and rejected.

**The target isn't a thousand cards. It's a thousand reasons to want to see who's at the door next.**

## 0. What the first human playtest taught us

> "It sure gets less fun as it goes… it starts with soup and naps and turns into approvals and disapprovals… we've lost the geography and territoriality… the comedy just isn't there."

**The diagnosis.** The early game is funny because it is concrete and physical: soup, naps, a garage, a lawnmower. As the movement grew, the writing became administrative: memos, motions, charters, approvals. Scale made it abstract when it should have made it bigger and stranger in more places.

**The rules from now on:**

1. **Every card happens somewhere.** It is about a real town, a real room, a real object. A national-era card is still about one hall, one bridge, one sea lion.
2. **Write the person on the road, not the policy.** The best comedy comes from *who you sent where*: Gary in a cathedral, Dez in a town with a noise ordinance, Agnes taking the minutes of a whole high street.
3. **Customs mutate, they don't get ruled on.** Let towns warp the belief ("they do it lying down"), and let the quarrels between versions be the jokes.
4. **No bureaucracy without a body.** A memo is only funny if Constance is standing outside the garage, unable to come in. The shelved cards (`"shelved": true`) are waiting for rewrites that obey this.
5. **Rivals are neighbours with grudges and addresses.** The Knitting Circle has a banner across the main road in Bingley; the North is Sunderland and Carlisle.

**The next writing priority** is *more road incidents*: per character, per era, and some per kind of town (rival turf, a big city, somewhere that does it differently). They carry the weekly loop. Today each character has 1–3; the target is 8–10 for the core cast.

## 1. The standard

- **Surprise without inconsistency.** We don't want Martin laminating something for the thousandth time. We want Martin doing something unexpected that makes perfect sense once you remember who he is: falling in love with the General Secretary through procedure; being the only person who knew where the fire exits were.
- **Three registers.** Some cards should be laugh-out-loud funny, some quietly ridiculous, and some simply good character writing that makes the next joke land. A batch made only of punchlines is exhausting.
- **Situations, not ideology.** We write the problem; the player's choices create the movement's identity. Cards refer to *their* belief (`{belief}`), *their* customs (`{tradition}`), *their* meeting day (`{day}`). No card decides what the movement believes.
- **Petty human motives at every scale.** In the National era, television, conventions and a national office are only the stage. The motive is still that Tobias wants to be taken seriously, Constance wants nothing to change, and Juniper wants the story to be as good as the one she grew up with.
- **Pay off the past.** A card that refers back to an earlier decision is worth three that don't (see consequential callback coverage, §7).

## 4. The National era: the movement becomes an institution

**The premise.** Somewhere around twenty thousand members, the movement stops being a group of people and becomes an *organisation*, with a national office, officers, regions, a convention and a brand. Everything the player decided in a garage now has a department.

**The four pressures**, from the brief:

1. **Local leaders decide without the founder.** Tobias's Northern Edicts; the regions; things you find out from the newsletter.
2. **National officers have competing priorities.** Constance (continuity) against Rolf (revenue) against Ophelia (growth) against Juniper (the culture).
3. **Members care more about their particular customs than the original purpose.** The custom lobbies, the Charter, and the mission statement that says only "To continue."
4. **Founders become figureheads.** Ribbon-cutting, the statue, finding out from the newsletter, the Emeritus rumour.

**Scale of absurdity.** The garage and laminated signs become television, bureaucracy, lanyards, conventions and the Heritage Register. The stakes on a card are larger: gauge moves of ±6–15, and growth by percentages, not handfuls. The motive behind each card stays as small as ever.

## 6. Premise bank and editorial record

### Batch 1: pitched, kept, cut

Seventy-four premises were pitched and 64 were written. The cuts are recorded so we don't pitch them again:

| Cut premise | Why |
|---|---|
| The founder's face on a banknote | Too big too soon. It belongs in the World era. |
| A rival movement steals the name | Already done (Agnes's *Original*, Rolf's company). Repeated premise. |
| Martin laminates the national office | The thing we promised not to do. |
| A celebrity joins | Generic. Replaced by the impostor, who gives better speeches than you. |
| Members unionise against the national office | Strong, but the National era already has three internal-power arcs. Held for batch 2. |
| Dez buys a blimp | A joke about money, not about Dez. Replaced by the truth on live TV. |
| The movement gets a theme song | Fine, but flat. Folded into Juniper ("knows the words to all the songs"). |
| A spy from a government agency | Tonal mismatch: paranoia is not this game's register. |
| Gary wins the lottery | No motive. Gary wouldn't buy a ticket. |
| A merger with a gym chain | Rolf has enough commerce cards. Held for when Rolf is gone (a payoff for `rolf_rival`). |
| The founder's mother writes in | Good, but it belongs to succession and legacy. Held. |
| Time-travel misunderstanding | Breaks the world's realism. Rejected. |

## 7. Editorial process for every batch

1. **Pitch** premises in a shared list: one line each, with stream, speaker and callback.
2. **Cut hard.** Reject a pitch if it:
   - repeats an existing premise;
   - fits the "known engine" column when it could fit the "surprising" one;
   - decides the movement's ideology for the player;
   - is mean to a real group;
   - would be funnier as a reaction line than as a card.
3. **Write** the batch in arcs first, then singles, then wildcards.
4. **Validate** with `npm run content:validate` (0 errors, 0 warnings).
5. **Stress** with `npm run content:stress` and `npm run content:relationships`. The batch should not:
   - lower consequential callback coverage;
   - add a shape that carries a playthrough (over 22%);
   - add dominated choices;
   - add inert choices;
   - give any character more departures from collateral damage.
6. **Read aloud.** Render 10 random playthroughs of the new cards with real tokens filled in, as the stress tooling does. Strike anything that reads wrong with real values.
7. **Human playtest.** Automated tests prove the engine works. Only a person can tell whether Martin is funny the fifteenth time. Each batch ends with one, using the *Playtesting* option on the start screen, which fast-forwards a new game to the start of the era under test.
